Think like a forecaster

Use the map, the slider, and the charts to answer these. Open each one to check your thinking.

1. Find the day Simon "blew up." How fast did it strengthen?

From Oct 9 at 8 AM to Oct 10 at 8 AM (Eastern), Simon's winds jumped from about 100 mph to 150 mph. Forecasters call an increase of 35 mph or more in 24 hours rapid intensification. Warm ocean water, moist air, and calm winds high in the atmosphere let the storm feed on itself.

2. Compare the wind chart and the pressure chart. What pattern do you see?

They are mirror images. When the pressure at the center was lowest (928 mb on Oct 11), the winds were strongest. Lower pressure in the middle pulls air inward harder, and that rushing air is the wind.

3. Since Oct 12, Simon has barely moved. Why would sitting still make a hurricane weaker?

A hurricane's winds stir up the ocean. When it stalls, it churns up cooler water from deeper down, right underneath itself. Cooler water means less heat and moisture to fuel the storm.

4. Why does the forecast cone get wider the farther ahead it goes?

Every small uncertainty grows over time. The Hurricane Center says its track forecasts are off by about 115 miles on average at day 4 and 145 miles at day 5. The center of the storm will probably stay inside the cone, but the storm's wind and rain can reach outside it.

5. Which 2026 storm had the lowest pressure, and how does that fit the pattern from question 2?

Hurricane Polo in September: its pressure fell to 892 mb, and it also had the season's strongest winds, about 180 mph (Category 5). The storm with the biggest pressure drop had the fastest winds, the same pattern you saw inside Simon.

6. Simon is a "very small" hurricane. How small?

Right now, hurricane-force winds reach only about 10 miles from the center, and tropical-storm-force winds about 35 miles. Look up a place 10 miles from your school. A storm like Simon could bring damaging winds there while a spot 50 miles away barely notices.
