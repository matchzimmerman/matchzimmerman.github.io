Tuned to 3105

US Coast Guard cutter Itasca, off Howland Island in the middle of the Pacific. 2 July 1937, a few minutes after six in the morning. You are on radio watch.

An airplane flown by Amelia Earhart, with Fred Noonan navigating, left New Guinea 18 hours ago for this island. She carries a radio for 500, 3105 and 6210 kilocycles. Keep the receiver tuned. Log everything you hear, exactly as you hear it.

You have a receiver, a plotting sheet, the sky to the east and a logbook. Work in pairs, with sound on. Time runs ten times faster than real time; you can pause it.

The record

What you heard is one thing. What survives is a US Navy report written in July 1937 after the search: Report of Earhart Search by U.S. Navy and U.S. Coast Guard, July 2–18, 1937, now in the National Archives. It summarizes the airplane's messages twice, in two different sections. Compare them to each other, and to your own log. Click any words that differ to add them to your notes.

Other things the Navy wrote down

"That the strength of radio signals in Itasca was greatest at 1928."
"GCT sunrise, Howland, on 2 July was 1745."
"That morning of 2 July Itasca was laying a heavy smoke screen which hung for hours."
"This was the last authentic message received from the airplane."

Notes for teachers

About 40 minutes, students in pairs, sound on, no logins. Nothing is explained up front and nothing is scored; the questions arrive in the logbook as the morning unfolds. The messages are as the Navy report summarizes them (§13), not the Itasca's own radio log; the record tab shows the second version. The sun is computed for Howland on 2 July 1937 (rises 17:46 GCT on a bearing of 66.9°; the report gives 1745). In the search, nothing is ever found, because nothing was. The Lexington's aircraft searched 151,556 square miles from 13 to 18 July 1937 (report p. 14). Progress saves in this browser only.
