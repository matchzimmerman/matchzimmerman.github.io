Report §13, p. 4

Why 157–337. The report itself gives sunrise at Howland that morning as 1745 GCT. Computed for 0°48′N 176°38′W, the sun clears the horizon at 17:46 on a bearing of 66.9°. A line of position from a sun sight runs at right angles to the sun's bearing: 156.9° and 336.9°. So a sunrise sun line matches the reported line to within a tenth of a degree. The same calculation at 2013 gives 153–333. A sun line tells you the direction you are on, not where along it you are. Every magenta line on the sheet is equally consistent with the message.
Source. US Navy, Report of Earhart Search by U.S. Navy and U.S. Coast Guard, July 2–18, 1937, file 080_PC31-22_146_07 in the National Archives' Earhart release, pages stamped NW 90261: "Estimate and Decisions", Known Facts items 17, 20–23 (p. 2), and §13 (pp. 3–4). Quotes were checked against the page images. These are the Navy's summaries, not the Itasca's radio log itself. Computed. Sun positions use a standard low-precision solar ephemeris (about 0.1° accuracy) for each minute at Howland. Island positions are rounded to the nearest minute of arc. The Itasca's mark is approximate: the report says only "immediately to northeastward of Howland". Not here. The airplane is never drawn, because no message gives a place. Distance rings are drawn around Howland because "out" names no bearing; the reports don't say which miles. Sound: radio static and a tone at 310.5 Hz, which is 3105 kilocycles scaled down ten thousand times. No voice.
