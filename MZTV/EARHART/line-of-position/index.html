Line of Position

Students become the radio room of the US Coast Guard cutter Itasca at Howland Island on the morning of 2 July 1937, waiting for Amelia Earhart and Fred Noonan. Every message that arrives is acted out in the room with their bodies. They learn what a piece of evidence can tell you, and what it can't, by standing inside it.

One class period, ages 14–18. Uses one projector and speaker, the students' phone compass apps, masking tape and a rope. No logins. Everything quoted is from a 1937 US Navy report; everything computed is labelled.

What students should come away with

  • A distance with no bearing is a circle. A bearing with no reference point is a line. Neither is a place.
  • The same event can be recorded twice by the same institution in different words, and both can be primary sources.
  • A navigator's line of position comes from the sun's direction. They can check it: a sunrise sun sight at Howland that morning gives exactly the reported 157–337.
  • Not finding something does not mean it isn't there.

Aligned to the C3 Framework for Social Studies: D2.His.10.9-12 (limitations of evidence), D2.His.11.9-12 (critiquing sources by maker, date and purpose), D2.His.16.9-12 (integrating evidence into a reasoned argument).

Set up the room (10 minutes before class)

  1. Howland. Put a tape X at the centre of the open floor. That's Howland Island.
  2. North. Open a phone compass app, set it to true north (iPhone: Settings › Compass › Use True North), and tape an arrow on the floor at the X pointing north. Label it N.
  3. Sunrise. With the compass, tape a second arrow from the X toward 67°, and label it SUNRISE 67°. That's where the sun came up at Howland that morning.
  4. The rope. Lay a rope circle around the X at the "100 miles" radius for your room (see scale). The walls are the 200-mile circle.
  5. Sound. Connect the projector's speaker. The static is quiet on purpose; set it so it's just audible at the back of the room.

Scale

ClassroomAbout 8 × 9 m. The walls are 200 miles out, about 4 m from the X. 1 m is about 50 miles. Rope circle: 2 m radius.
GymAbout 25 m across. Mark a 200-mile circle 12 m from the X with cones. 1 m is about 16 miles. Rope: 6 m radius.
FieldUse 1 m = 4 miles. The 200-mile circle is 50 m out, and the walk along the line in cue 10 can last the whole minute.

Run of show (50 minutes)

ClockMessage or momentThe room does

The heart of the lesson is cue 6: "30 minutes gas remaining" starts a 30-minute countdown that runs in real time while the class works. The next messages arrive after it reaches zero. Let that be felt; don't explain it in advance.

Questions for the end

  1. Stand where you were when the last message came. Could anyone in the room prove they were in the wrong place?
  2. The Navy wrote the 7:42 message as "30 minutes gas remaining" and also as "one-half hour fuel". What else changed between the two versions? Why might the same office write it down twice?
  3. Messages kept arriving for 43 minutes after the half hour ran out. What does that tell you about the fuel estimate, or about the messages?
  4. The Navy said the line "presumably passed through Howland Island". What would you need to know to check that presumption?
  5. If you had planes for three days, where would you search, and what would it mean if you found nothing?

Exit ticket. One sentence each: something the evidence shows, something it can't show, and one source you'd want next.

Block period: the search (40 more minutes)

  1. While students are out, hide a coin somewhere on the floor within the 200-mile circle.
  2. Lights off. Teams of three get one flashlight and three minutes. The flashlight must point straight down from waist height: that's their sweep width.
  3. Each team marks the strip it searched on a floor sketch. Most teams will miss the coin even inside their strip.
  4. Afterwards, ask whether a strip searched without finding anything is a strip where the coin isn't. In 1937 the Lexington's aircraft alone searched 151,556 square miles between 13 and 18 July (report, Summary of Operations, p. 14). Nothing was found.

Sources, and what is real

Quotes were checked against the page images. These are the Navy's summaries of what the Itasca received, not the Itasca's own radio log.

Computed. Sun positions use a standard low-precision solar ephemeris (about 0.1°). For Howland on 2 July 1937 it gives sunrise at 17:46 GCT on a bearing of 66.9°. The report gives sunrise as 1745. A line of position from that sun sight runs 156.9°/336.9°.
