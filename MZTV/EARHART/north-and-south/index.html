North and South

Lockheed Electra NR16020, over the Pacific.
2 July 1937, 6:10 in the morning, Howland time.

You left New Guinea nineteen hours ago. Howland Island is somewhere ahead: a strip of sand two kilometres long, a few metres above the sea. A Coast Guard cutter is waiting there with its radio.

Sound on. Drag to look around. Turn with the arrow keys, A and D, or by dragging near the bottom of the window. Hold Transmit when it lights up.

08:55 Howland

"This was the last authentic message received from the airplane."

US Navy, report of the search, July 1937. Nobody knows what happened next.

What's real

The words. Everything you transmit is what the plane sent, at the minute it sent it, as written down in the US Navy's 1937 report of the search (National Archives; §13 and Known Facts 17, pages stamped NW 90261). The cutter heard her. She did not hear answers: the report notes "no answers to questions put to Earhart."

The sky. The sun is computed for this place and morning. It rises at 06:16 Howland time on a bearing of 67°, about 11° left of a course to Howland, so you fly into it.

The island. Howland and Baker are where they are, at their real size. You see Howland from about 15 nautical miles in good light, less into the glare.

Not known. Where the plane really was. Each flight starts you somewhere different, 180–220 nautical miles out, believing you are on course. Most flights never see the island. If yours does, it was luck the record doesn't give her.

Made up for this. Clouds and their shadows, the sea, the engine sound, the speed of time (six times real time), the cockpit. Your altitude, 1,000 feet, is an assumption. Nothing here shows how the flight ended, because nobody knows.

The forecast she carried (Known Facts 16): "175° E to Howland; ENE 15 and squalls to be detoured."
