THE WORLD, BEFORE THE INTERVENTION
These are yeast cells.

Yeast is a microscopic, single-celled fungus. Each small oval in these fields represents one simulated yeast cell. They divide and produce descendants.

ADE2 is a gene in their DNA.

It helps yeast make adenine, a molecule cells need. Changing ADE2 may affect growth and pigment — not always in the same way.

Color can reveal — or conceal — change.

When ADE2 is disrupted, some yeast become reddish if the surrounding adenine is limited. With plenty available, they may look pale even though their DNA has changed.

WORDS FROM INSIDE THE WORLD / OPEN WHEN NEEDED

What is CRISPR actually doing?

CRISPR is a family of tools for making changes to DNA. In a common form, a guide RNA helps an enzyme called Cas9 find a specific DNA region and cut it. When the cell repairs that cut, a gene may stop working or be changed. This artwork only models the resulting functional state; it does not show the real cutting and repair chemistry.

What is a cell? Is this what yeast really looks like?

Yeast is a fungus made of single cells. Budding yeast reproduces by growing a smaller daughter cell from a parent. The ovals here stand for individual yeast cells and their descendants, but their placement, movement, and timing are artistic simulations rather than microscope footage.

What do ADE2+, ADE2−, DNA, and genome mean?

DNA carries inherited biological information; the genome is an organism’s full collection of DNA. A gene is one region of that information. ADE2 is the name of a yeast gene needed for a step in making adenine. In this world, ADE2+ means the gene works, and ADE2− means its function is disrupted. These are state labels, not real DNA-letter sequences.

What is adenine, and why does food change the color?

Adenine is a molecule cells use in DNA, RNA, and other essential chemistry. Yeast normally makes it through a chain of reactions that involves ADE2. In some yeast strains with broken ADE2, a reddish pigment can accumulate when available adenine is limited. With abundant adenine, the red appearance may fade even though the gene remains broken.

What is the difference between a control, an edit, and a descendant?

The control (left) is the population that never receives the editing pulse. The intervention (right) starts the same but can be altered. A descendant is a new cell produced by an earlier cell. In this simplified world, descendants copy their parent's ADE2 state, allowing the effects of one intervention to spread through a family of cells.

What do the percentages, pulses, and beats mean?

“Gene switched off” is the fraction of right-side cells whose ADE2 is disrupted. “Reddish pigment” is an average modeled color intensity, not the number of visibly red cells. A pulse is a fictional way to represent reaching some cells. Beats represent simulation steps, not seconds, minutes, or laboratory times. The modeled probabilities are invented and must not be interpreted as CRISPR success rates.

04 / WHAT THIS WORLD IS

This is an artwork built around a real genetic relationship: ADE2 function, adenine availability and red pigmentation in budding yeast. A pulse models the idea that editing reaches some cells but not others. Descendants inherit the altered state. The control and intervention fields can diverge over time.

Time, cell behavior, targeting probability, geometry and sound are interpretive choices, not calibrated experimental measurements. The dots are artistic cell proxies, not a faithful rendering of yeast colonies or a representation of edit success rates in a real experiment.

05 / WHERE THE BIOLOGY ENTERS

Published yeast experiments demonstrate that disrupting ADE2 with CRISPR can create red colonies. ADE2 disruption also affects adenine synthesis, so supplementation can change both growth and visible pigment. The simulation preserves those qualitative relationships without claiming to predict a particular experiment.
