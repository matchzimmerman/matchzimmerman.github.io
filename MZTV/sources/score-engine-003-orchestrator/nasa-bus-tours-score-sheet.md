## Principles used

- **Theme and development.** One short melody carries the whole piece. Long pieces feel unified because the listener keeps recognising it as it changes.
- **Augmentation / diminution.** Playing a melody slower or faster. Slower reads as weightier or more distant.
- **Inversion.** Turning the melody upside down: every step up becomes a step down. Still recognisable by its rhythm.
- **Sequence.** Repeating a phrase at a higher or lower pitch. Builds momentum without new material.
- **Fragmentation and liquidation.** Using only part of the theme, then fewer and fewer notes, until it dissolves. A natural way to end.
- **Call and response (antecedent / consequent).** A phrase that ends open, answered by one that closes. Passing the answer to another family adds contrast.
- **Countermelody and contrary motion.** A second line that moves against the first, so both stay audible.
- **Colour doubling and octave doubling.** Two instruments on one line make a new timbre; an octave apart they add weight and brightness.
- **Crescendo by orchestration.** Building intensity by adding families one at a time, rather than just turning up.
- **Texture contrast.** Thinning to a single line after a full section makes both feel stronger.
- **Pedal point.** A held bass note under moving harmony: tension that wants release.
- **Cadence.** A chord progression that closes a phrase or section. In phrygian, the defining close is the half step from E♭ down to D.
- **Spacing by the overtone series.** Wide intervals low (root, fifth, octave), close intervals high, so chords sound open rather than muddy.
- **Phrase swell.** Each four-bar phrase rises toward its third bar and breathes in the fourth.
