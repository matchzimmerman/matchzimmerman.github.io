MZTV · SCORE ENGINE 001 · SLO-FUNK-DUB

The film plays the band.

A music visualizer listens to sound and makes pictures. This does the opposite. It watches a piece of film, measures what is happening on screen frame by frame, and uses those measurements to play a slow dub-funk band in real time. You never hear the film. You hear the band reading it.

The flip
Usual
sound→analysis→pictures

The bass hits, the screen pulses. The image decorates the music.

Here
pictures→analysis→sound

The edit cuts, the band drops out. The music answers the image.

What happens, in four steps

IN

A clip plays

Any video file, dropped into the page. It is read on your own computer and never uploaded.

LOOK

The engine watches

Every frame is shrunk to a 160 × 90 grid of pixels and compared to the one before. Twelve measurements come out.

DECIDE

Rules interpret

A set of plain rules turns those measurements into musical decisions: tempo, who plays, which chords, where things sit in the stereo field.

OUT

The band plays

Bass, kick, rim, skank guitar, horn and strings, synthesised live in the browser, always in time and in key.

What it actually measures

None of this is "understanding" the film. The engine doesn't know a face from a car. It only measures change, light and colour in a tiny grid of pixels, which turns out to be enough to follow how a scene moves.

MotionHow many pixels changed since the last frame, and how much.
Where the motion isThe centre point of all that change: left, right, up, down.
Camera movementWhether the whole picture is sliding one way, the way it does when a camera pans.
CutsA sudden jump in the overall mix of light and dark tones, far beyond normal motion. That is what an edit looks like to a machine.
ImpactsA burst of motion well above what the scene was just doing: a punch, a door, a fall.
FlashesA sudden jump in brightness.
StillnessAlmost nothing changing for a second and a half.
Light and darkAverage brightness of the frame, and its contrast.
WarmthWhether the colours lean red and orange or blue.
DetailHow much fine texture there is: grass, crowds and rain score high, sky scores low.
PaceLong-run motion plus how often the film cuts.
TensionMotion, contrast and cutting rate combined into one number.

When the film does this, the band does that

On screenIn the musicWhy that pairing
A cutDub-out: one hit from the whole band, flung into a long echo, then near silence. If the cut lands late in a bar, the downbeat moves onto the cut.An edit is a break in time. Dub's version of a break is dropping the band and letting the echo carry. Moving the "one" onto the cut makes the music reorganise around the editing.
An impactA kick (big hits) or rim shot (light hits), snapped to the next sixteenth note.Physical hits on screen become percussion. Snapping to the grid keeps it a groove, not a sound effect.
StillnessThe rhythm section steps out. Strings and echoes hold.A still frame is negative space. The music leaves room instead of filling it. Movement brings the band back.
A busy sceneFewer parts drop out, and the parts slowly grow more notes.More happening on screen means a fuller, denser arrangement. Quiet scenes thin it out.
Overall paceTempo, between 46 and 60 beats per minute.Fast-cut, high-motion footage pushes the tempo up. Slow footage lets it sink. It changes gradually, over about eight seconds.
DarknessDarker chords, longer echoes, a bigger room.Dark scenes move the band to its lowest, most dissonant vamp and let the reverb open up.
WarmthBrighter, more open strings.Warm, saturated images open the filter on the strings. Cold ones keep them muffled.
Camera movementThe echoes slide across the stereo field in the same direction.When the camera pans left, the sound drifts left with it.
Where the motion isHorn and guitar pan toward that side.Action on the right of frame pulls those parts right.
DetailA low hiss bed under the band.Visual texture becomes audible texture, kept low and dark.
A flashA single horn note thrown into the echo.A burst of light becomes a burst of sound that hangs in the air.
Whatever dominatesTakes the lead. Everything else ducks under it.A moving camera gives the lead to the guitar, heavy motion to the bass, stillness or darkness to the strings, otherwise the horn. The rest dips in volume each time the lead plays, so the lead always reads clearly.

Why it sometimes lines up perfectly

Films are already rhythmic

Editors cut to a pulse, and action has its own timing. Because every musical decision comes from the same pixels, the band inherits that structure. Its pacing and mood tend to follow the scene without anyone scoring it.

The data is arbitrary on purpose

Pixel counts and colour averages don't mean anything musical by themselves. The rules give them meaning. That is the experiment: treat a film as a data source, then see when an arbitrary reading starts to feel intentional.

Always in time, always in key

Every hit is snapped to the beat and every note is chosen from the key, so even a wrong guess sounds like part of the arrangement. When a cut or impact lands right on the grid, it feels scored.

The same sound engine first ran in RESIDUE GROOVE, an MZTV broadcast piece played by an invisible external data stream. Swapping that stream for a camera's-eye reading of film is the branch.
